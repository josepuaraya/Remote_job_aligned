InSAR is a common technique used to measure surface deformation, applied to different topics. In volcanology it lets us estimate the ground displacement produced by volcanic inflation, the rate of that inflation, and, importantly, the location, depth and volume of the magmatic source that produces it -- information that is vital to define a plan of action at an observatory.

## Atmospheric noise: exclusion vs. repair

InSAR data is affected by atmospheric noise. Some of that noise is correlated with elevation (a tropospheric delay effect) and can be corrected with a phase-versus-elevation regression; other noise is more turbulent and cannot be meaningfully repaired that way. Applying the elevation correction automatically is a mistake: it can remove part of the real deformation signal along with the atmospheric artifact, particularly where topography and the source's surface expression happen to correlate. The correction is therefore only applied to an interferogram when it demonstrably reduces the residual against the currently fitted source model, compared to leaving the data raw; if neither the raw nor the corrected version brings the residual within an acceptable range, the interferogram is excluded rather than force-fit. This decision is made per interferogram, and the decision, the fit, and the model update are repeated until the set of included/excluded interferograms stabilizes -- a single pass is not enough, since excluding or repairing one interferogram shifts the fitted model that the next interferogram's decision is judged against.

This is not a marginal concern in this dataset: the topography is deliberately realistic, with its high point near the true source (a volcano's own edifice typically sits above its reservoir), so elevation and the real deformation signal are genuinely correlated rather than independent. This was checked directly: an implementation that applies the elevation regression automatically, without gating it against the fitted model's residual, recovers a source with a meaningfully and consistently worse volume estimate than the properly-gated approach, across independent realizations of the same scenario -- confirming this is a real, substantial risk from the topography's placement, not just a theoretical one, while the properly-gated approach still comfortably recovers the true source with a healthy margin.

Beyond simple magnitude, atmospheric noise is also spatially correlated -- nearby points on the ground tend to share similar atmospheric delay, rather than each point being an independent draw. Treating every point as independent and equally weighted, when the noise is actually correlated, understates how much genuinely independent information the data carries and produces an overconfident (too narrow) uncertainty estimate. The noise's spatial correlation structure is therefore estimated directly from the screened residuals (an empirical semivariogram approach, restricted to the range of separations where the estimate is not confounded by other structure in the data) and used to properly weight the interferograms relative to each other in the joint inversion, rather than assuming independence.

Not every defect in an interferogram is atmospheric. A processing artifact -- a discrete offset affecting only part of an interferogram's footprint, of the kind produced by a phase-unwrapping error -- is a different failure mode from smooth atmospheric noise, and needs a different diagnostic: rather than a regression against a continuous covariate like elevation, it calls for testing whether some spatial split of the interferogram's points, corrected by removing the offset between the two sides, brings the residual down substantially. A genuine discrete defect responds sharply to this test; ordinary smooth noise does not, and this distinction matters -- a correction method flexible enough to fit any residual pattern will eventually "explain away" genuine noise too, quietly eroding the exclusion criterion for the interferograms that truly cannot be repaired. So the correction is only accepted when the offset it finds is of a magnitude consistent with a genuine discrete defect, not merely whatever incrementally reduces the residual.

A related failure mode showed up empirically, through the same kind of testing: noise intended to be genuinely unrepairable has to resist a broad range of smooth correction attempts, not just the specific elevation-regression and discrete-offset methods described above. A smooth, low-order spatial trend is exactly the kind of feature a general polynomial or bilinear surface fit can absorb, regardless of what specific method is used to find it -- if the "unrepairable" noise itself happens to look like such a trend, a sufficiently flexible smooth-surface correction can legitimately drive the residual down no matter how it's parameterized, even though the underlying atmospheric turbulence is not actually structured that way. This was caught the same way as the discrete-defect false positive above: by testing the noise's realism against a genuinely flexible correction, rather than only against the specific methods this pipeline happens to apply. The generator was corrected so that genuinely unrepairable noise has fine-scale spatial structure that a smooth trend surface cannot meaningfully capture, rather than resembling a large-scale ramp or gradient that any general trend-fitting approach would defeat.

Excluding a discrete-defect interferogram is not a safe default either. A processing artifact of this kind is, by construction, repairable -- discarding it instead of diagnosing and correcting it throws away genuinely usable deformation data, not merely a missed opportunity. Empirically, this only shows up as a measurable cost when it involves enough of the data: with a single such interferogram among many otherwise-usable ones, whether it is repaired or discarded has only a small, noise-dominated effect on the final answer, run to run. With several, the effect is real and consistent across independent realizations of the same scenario -- the reported volume change in particular comes out meaningfully worse when they are discarded rather than repaired. So this is checked directly: every discrete-defect interferogram must actually be repaired and carried into the final inversion, not merely excluded alongside the genuinely unrepairable ones.

## Source inversion

The source is modeled as a single Mogi (1958) point source, characterized by its horizontal location, depth, and volume change. The inversion works directly with the raw line-of-sight (LOS) displacement measured by each interferogram, combining both ascending and descending tracks, rather than trying to isolate the vertical component alone -- LOS geometry has real sensitivity to north-south motion (a consequence of the satellites' near-polar orbit), and discarding that sensitivity by assuming vertical-only motion introduces a modeling bias, not just added noise. The onset day of inflation and the linear (constant-rate) form of the post-onset deformation are treated as known, disclosed quantities rather than free parameters to fit -- estimating the onset timing and the deformation's temporal shape jointly with the spatial source parameters is a substantially harder, more weakly identified problem, and is out of scope for this exercise.

The acquisition schedule includes a genuine multi-week gap with no data coverage at all; the interferogram spanning that gap has a correspondingly longer duration and carries proportionally more information about the cumulative deformation during the unobserved period. The joint inversion is built to combine interferograms of varying duration on this basis, rather than relying on any single, uniformly-sampled interferogram.

## Independent validation against GNSS

InSAR's own spatial sampling, relative to the source's depth, means the data alone only weakly separates the source's depth from its cumulative volume change -- a well-known trade-off in this style of inversion. A source estimate can show small InSAR residuals while still sitting on the wrong point along that trade-off. A continuous GNSS network gives an independent, non-InSAR measurement of the same deformation, unaffected by atmospheric delay, and is used two ways: first, as an additional dataset in the joint inversion (weighted, like InSAR, according to its own noise structure -- GNSS noise here has both an independent per-station component and a common-mode component shared across the network on a given day, which must likewise be estimated from the data and accounted for, rather than assumed away); and second, as a check the final answer must survive -- the fitted source's predicted displacement at the network's primary station must reconcile with that station's own observed record. A fit that only minimizes InSAR residuals, without genuinely incorporating the independent GNSS evidence, is not treated as a complete answer.

## Predictive validation

Fitting a model to the data used to constrain it says nothing about whether that model generalizes -- a calibration that matches the interferograms and GNSS records it was built from can still be wrong in a way those same records cannot reveal. A separate observation, held out from the fitting process entirely, is required to check that: the fitted model's prediction at this held-out observation must be reported and must agree with what was actually measured there. Because this observation played no part in constraining the fit, agreement is a genuine, independent check on whether the recovered source transfers to unseen data, not a restatement of how well the model fits what it was given.

## Uncertainty quantification

The 95% confidence intervals for the source parameters are derived via the delta method from the local curvature (Jacobian) of the final joint, properly-weighted inversion, rather than from the earlier, unweighted fit -- an interval computed before accounting for the data's correlation structure understates the true uncertainty. Reported intervals are required to be internally consistent (well-formed, and containing their own point estimate) and of a realistic width for this kind of joint geophysical inversion -- neither so narrow as to be an overconfident artifact of ignoring correlated noise, nor so wide as to be uninformative.

## Validation approach

The full pipeline -- noise-covariance estimation, screening and correction decisions (including discrete-defect detection), joint inversion, and uncertainty quantification -- was validated by generating multiple independent synthetic realizations of the same underlying scenario (varying only the random noise draw) and confirming, across all of them, that: the atmospheric-noise screening correctly separates genuinely unrepairable interferograms from clean and repairable ones, without the discrete-defect correction method spuriously absorbing genuine unrepairable noise; the recovered source location, depth and volume change land close to the true, known values used to generate the synthetic data; the reported confidence intervals are well-formed and self-consistent; the estimated noise-covariance structure reflects genuine engagement with the data rather than a placeholder value; the model's prediction at the GNSS network reconciles with that network's own record; and the model's prediction at the held-out validation observation agrees with what was actually measured there. Deliberately unreasonable submissions (for example, values copied from raw input files rather than derived from the fitted model, or placeholder/implausible parameter values) were also checked to confirm they are correctly rejected, so that the acceptance criteria are neither trivially satisfiable nor unfairly strict against a genuinely sound, differently-implemented approach. A specific case worth noting: an early version of the discrete-defect correction method was flexible enough that it occasionally found a spurious partial correction for an interferogram that was genuinely unrepairable, nearly pushing it below the exclusion threshold for the wrong reason -- this was caught by the same multi-realization validation and fixed by requiring the correction to be of a magnitude consistent with a genuine discrete defect, not merely whatever incrementally helped.

## Output generation

`result.txt` is produced as a single JSON file summarizing the pipeline's outputs: the fitted source location, depth, and cumulative volume change with their 95% confidence intervals; the Poisson's ratio assumed; which interferograms were screened out, corrected, or used in the final inversion; the model's own predicted displacement at the GNSS network's primary station, for reconciliation; the estimated InSAR and GNSS noise-covariance parameters used to weight the joint inversion; the model's own predicted displacement at the held-out validation observation, for predictive-validation reconciliation; and a sampled vertical/east-west decomposition of the fitted deformation field, as a descriptive summary of the source's surface expression.
